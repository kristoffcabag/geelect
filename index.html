Understanding Philippine Popular Culture in Cartoons

Adarna: The Mythical Bird (1997)

Creator context: Rooted in the Filipino korido Ibong Adarna; the 1997 animated film was directed by Geraldo A. Garccia. Audience: children and families, and students who meet the story in school literature.

Description: Prince Juan journeys to capture the enchanted bird whose song heals the ailing king, and is repeatedly wronged by his jealous brothers.

Values reflected: utang na loob (Juan’s debt to his father and to those who help him), family loyalty, resilience through hardship, and the cycle of betrayal and forgiveness. Reconciliation is the ending, which says something about how Filipinos prefer to restore a family rather than discard it.

Ang Panday

Creator context: Created in comics by Carlo J. Caparas and Steve Gan, made iconic on film by Fernando Poe Jr., and adapted as a 1986 animated series directed by Gerry A. Garcia. Audience: children, teens and the action-fantasy audience across generations.

Description: Flavio, a humble blacksmith, forges a mystical sword from a fallen star and uses it against tyrants and dark forces.

Values reflected: working-class heroism, bayanihan (villagers rise together), labor turned into courage, and standing up to oppression. The hero comes from the forge, not the palace, so power belongs to ordinary workers.

How cartoons shape children

Cartoons are early teachers. Children absorb attitudes before they can argue with them: from Adarna that dishonesty and envy wreck families, and from Panday that courage is a duty to the weak. They also shape perception, since a child who sees folklore animated learns it as ours. Repetition makes those values feel natural, so cartoons can model pakikipagkapwa, but they can also normalize violence as the only answer to injustice.

Personal insight

As an information systems student, I see cartoons as the first “interface” to Filipino culture. They preserve stories that textbooks make dull, they promote them to new audiences, and they evolve them when new retellings change the art style, platform and heroes. The danger is that if local animation stays scarce, imported shows will define what children find normal. Supporting Filipino animation is how we keep Juan and Flavio from becoming museum pieces.

Filipino and Foreign Performers

Frank Ely — “Pwede Ka Ba?”

Background: A young Filipino singer-songwriter and guitarist in the indie/OPM scene, known for intimate, guitar-led songs.

Foreign influences: Western soul and classic R&B grooves, bedroom/indie-pop production, and a retro, warm tone. These show in jazzy chord colors, relaxed vocal phrasing that sits slightly behind the beat, and a stripped hollow-body guitar style on stage.

How they are incorporated: the foreign palette is a frame, not a replacement. He plays soulful chords under a vocal that stays conversational, so the arrangement sounds global while the delivery stays close.

Filipino identity: Tagalog lyricism, harana-style sincerity (the question in the title is a modern serenade), and the kilig and hugot sensibility of wanting someone while being unsure of the answer.

Daniel Caesar — “Best Part”

Background: Canadian R&B singer-songwriter whose gospel upbringing and spare arrangements define his sound.

Characteristics and messages: vulnerability, gratitude, spiritual roots, minimalism and devotion. The song’s soft guitar and closeness make love sound like a quiet promise, not a show.

Influence on my philosophy: “Best Part” taught me that love is patient and often unspectacular. I try to apply that in my relationships, taking time before reacting and valuing the person who stays through ordinary days. His live sets, with almost no spectacle, remind me to stay grounded and let sincerity do the work. The song’s gratitude also pushes me to say thank you to my family and friends more often than I do.

Closing reflection

Filipino and foreign performers build popular culture together. Foreign artists bring forms, techniques and standards that local artists absorb; Filipino artists translate them into Tagalog, local feeling and local stages. Frank Ely and Daniel Caesar meet in the same emotional place, tenderness, from two different histories. Popular culture grows when influence becomes conversation instead of imitation.

Critiquing Panabo City: Marxism and Functionalism

Nabó is a themed lifestyle café offering crafted coffee, an aesthetic space for study and hangouts, and photo-ready interiors. Free Concert every Araw refers to the free community concerts of Araw ng Panabo, with local and guest performers, a public stage, food stalls and vendors. Expanding my Midterm Activity 2, I treat both as “experiences” sold or staged, not just products.

A. Marxist perspective

Ownership and control: Nabó is privately owned, so the owners control pricing, space and profit. Araw ng Panabo is controlled by the local government, with sponsors and event organizers shaping the program and branding.

Who benefits: café owners gain revenue and reputation; sponsors gain visibility; officials gain popularity. Baristas and staff earn wages while producing the surplus. Concert-goers receive entertainment but little financial return.

Commodity fetishism: the café sells a curated aesthetic, lifestyle and social-media image far beyond the coffee. The price hides the labor behind each cup. False consciousness: believing that trendy consumption equals status or happiness, so students spend allowance on the image of belonging.

Access: not equal. Purchasing power decides who can sit in Nabó; farmers, laborers and low-income families are less likely to. The free concert is more open, yet sponsor branding still turns the crowd into an audience for advertisers.

B. Functionalist perspective

Economy: the café circulates consumer spending and buys from local suppliers; Araw ng Panabo lifts vendors, food stalls and transport earnings.

Livelihood: baristas, service staff, sound and stage technicians, local artists and street vendors earn income.

Community: both spaces encourage interaction, local tourism and civic pride. The café is a study and social hub; the concert gathers neighbors across barangays.

Stability: they promote social cohesion, stress relief for students and workers, and shared local identity. The free concerts produce collective effervescence, the shared energy of singing and cheering together, which renews belonging to Panabo.

Critical assessment

My position is that Panabo’s experience economy benefits the community unevenly. The Araw ng Panabo concerts come closest to serving everyone because they are free and public, and their effervescence is real. Nabó gives genuine jobs and a place for students like me, but its benefits concentrate with owners and customers who can afford the experience. Both views are right at once: the system integrates people and still ranks them by income. Better outcomes would come from fair wages, buying from local suppliers, and keeping public events free of heavy commercial branding.
